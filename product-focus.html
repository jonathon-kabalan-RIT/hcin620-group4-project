Working Title:

Older Adults' Ability to Distinguish AI-Generated and Human-Created Online Content

Problem Statement:

Artificial intelligence is becoming increasingly integrated into everyday digital experiences, and older adults are interacting with AI chatbots for social connection, assistance, and engagement (Wu et al. 2026). However, frequent interaction with AI does not necessarily mean that older adults understand how AI works or how to evaluate the content it produces. Research shows that older adults are interested in learning about AI but may experience difficulties understanding AI concepts and prefer practical, hands-on approaches to learning (Tang Kang Jie et al. 2025).


This becomes more concerning as AI-generated content becomes increasingly realistic. Zhang (2026) found that older adults could be confident when identifying AI-generated health video presenters but often relied on inconsistent cues when making their judgments. This suggests that confidence does not always mean reliable identification of AI-generated content. In addition, research on explainable AI found that older users can experience difficulty with complex explanations and prefer information that is simplified and visually intuitive (Bezzaoui et al. 2025).


The problem can have real-world consequences because AI-generated media can be used for impersonation and scams. Research on deepfake scams found that AI-generated audio and visual impersonations can undermine traditional strategies used by older adults to recognize fraudulent content (LaRubbio et al. 2025). As AI-generated content becomes more realistic, older adults may face increasing difficulty determining whether the content they encounter online is authentic or AI-generated.

Solution:

We propose designing an accessible AI-content literacy tool for older adults that helps users evaluate whether digital content may be AI-generated or human-created.


The tool could allow users to upload or share online content, such as images, videos, audio, or text, and receive a simple assessment with an easy-to-understand explanation of the cues contributing to that assessment. Rather than only displaying an “AI” or “Human” label, the tool would use simple language and intuitive visual indicators to explain the reasoning.


The experience could also include short interactive learning activities that teach older adults how to recognize common indicators of AI-generated content. This hands-on approach aligns with research showing that older adults prefer practical approaches to learning about AI (Tang Kang Jie et al. 2025).


The goal is not simply to provide an AI detector, but to help older adults develop their own skills for evaluating potentially AI-generated content and make more informed decisions online.

Stakeholders/Users:

Potential users:

Older adults who regularly use digital platforms such as social media, messaging applications, video platforms, or other online information sources.


Stakeholders:

-> Older adults and their families
-> Digital-literacy educators and community organizations
-> Organizations that provide services and support to older adults
-> Designers and developers of digital platforms
-> Researchers studying AI literacy, misinformation, and aging
